Type नमस्ते into a phone video editor, pick a letter-by-letter animation, and press play. न arrives. म arrives. Then स shows up alone with a little slash hanging off it, and a vowel sign floats in a beat later with nothing to hold on to.

For a split second your greeting is spelled wrong, in front of everyone who watches the Reel.

This post is the one I wish I'd read before I shipped the first version of text animation in [Vits](https://vits.vevaar.com), my video editor for Android. It explains why Hindi breaks, what the fix is, and why the fix is only half the job.

## A Hindi letter is not one character

To a computer, नमस्ते is six Unicode characters:

- न (na)
- म (ma)
- स (sa)
- ् (the virama, the sign that kills the vowel)
- त (ta)
- े (the vowel sign e)

A Hindi reader sees three letters: न, म and स्ते. The virama glues स to त into the conjunct स्त, and the vowel sign sits on top of the pair. That combined shape is one letter in the reader's eye, even though it took four characters to type.

An animation that moves "one character at a time" therefore animates things that are not letters at all. Here is the same word cut three ways:

## The rule most phones are missing

The Unicode standard fixed this in version 15.1, released in September 2023. It added a rule (GB9c in the text segmentation spec) that says consonants joined by a virama form one cluster. With it, क्ष, स्त्र and a reph like र्क each count as one letter.

The catch is that a phone's text segmentation comes from the ICU library baked into its Android version, and the phones most people in India actually use shipped before that rule existed. Ask the system where the letters are and it gives you the middle row.

So Vits does it by hand. The whole splitter is about 80 lines of Kotlin, and the part that matters is one condition:

```kotlin
// a consonant right after a virama joins the cluster before it
prev in VIRAMAS && Character.isLetter(cp) -> true
```

`VIRAMAS` holds the virama of eight scripts: Devanagari, Bengali, Gurmukhi, Gujarati, Oriya, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam. The rest of the file handles the usual cases, like vowel signs, zero-width joiners, emoji skin tones and flag pairs, so they stay attached to their base too.

## Cutting it right is half the job

Once you know where the letters are, the obvious next move is to draw each one by itself and animate the pieces. That breaks Hindi a second way.

Devanagari letters change shape depending on their neighbours. Conjunct forms, half forms and where a vowel sign sits are all decided by the text shaper looking at the whole word. Draw स्ते without the rest of the line and the shaper may pick a different form. Draw every letter separately and the shirorekha, the line that runs along the top of the word, picks up tiny seams wherever two pieces meet.

Vits avoids both problems by never drawing a letter alone:

1. The whole text is laid out once by Android's own text stack (Minikin with HarfBuzz), so it shapes exactly as the system would.
2. Each piece is drawn into a texture atlas with its whole line as shaping context, so स्ते gets the form it would have in the full word.
3. During the animation every piece is one quad on the GPU that moves, turns, blurs or glows. The letter shapes themselves never get re-shaped mid-flight.

When the animation settles, the pieces have to add back up to the plain text. An automated check in the Vits repo renders both and compares them pixel by pixel, and they match at 51 to 53 dB PSNR. That is well past the point where a difference can be seen, so there is no seam through the headline.

### Why do conjuncts break in other editors?

Most editors split text by Unicode characters or by older grapheme rules, which separate consonants joined by a virama. Unicode 15.1 fixed the rule in 2023, but the text libraries on most Android phones in use are older than that.
